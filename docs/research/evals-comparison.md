# Ollie's evals against open-source eval practice and adaptive-learning research

**Researched:** 2026-09-17, against `src/evals/*.ts`, `src/cli/eval.ts`, `docs/evals/`, `docs/submission/`, `.scratch/k5-math/`, and primary sources fetched from OpenAI, UK AISI, promptfoo, Braintrust, LangChain, DeepEval, Ragas, Anthropic, arXiv and PMC. Every claim about an outside tool is cited to a page actually fetched.

## Summary

Ollie's harness is **structurally ahead of most eval work of its size**: a deterministic validator runs before any model-graded score, the model's *rejected* attempts are scored as well as its accepted ones, a real control arm (the Baseline) runs on identical seeds, two Simulated Learners are held out of prompt tuning, and the Judge's numbers are **withheld** until it agrees with hand-labelled work. Nothing in OpenAI Evals, Inspect AI, promptfoo or Braintrust gates on judge calibration that way, and none of them score rejected generations. What a rigorous reviewer would attack is **statistical power, not design**: every sample is between 6 and 30 items, the gate is decided on 20 and 10 single-labelled items, detection is a hand-written regex, the Simulated Learners cannot learn, and the committed report is still a `--fake` run — so the headline numbers measure the seams, not Claude. Against adaptive-learning research the picture is mixed in Ollie's favour: shipping an unvalidated simulation is the norm rather than the exception (half the simulated-learner literature offers no validity evidence at all), but every comparable product that has published an evaluation measured a **behavioural** outcome, not a rubric. The fixes are mostly arithmetic and cost a day each.

## 1. What the seven scores measure

- **Evidence Integrity** (`hypotheses.ts:183-255, 305-351`) — every Problem ID every Hypothesis cited, checked against the Log: the ID must exist *and* its Assistance State must agree with the claim's polarity (a "struggles / needs a Hint" claim must cite non-first-try-correct Problems, a "solid / no Hints" claim first-try-correct ones; refuted or neutral claims are checked for the ID only). Polarity comes from word lists with a negation rule. Scored over **every Notes the Coach wrote, rejected attempts included**.
- **Detection / Sessions to detection** (`hypotheses.ts:162-186`) — a planted weakness is detected in the first Session where a *supported* Hypothesis says the pattern in words, matched by per-weakness regexes (`crosses ten`, `bridg…10`, `regroup`; `missing addend`, `how many more`); naming only the Skill does not count. **False positives**: supported Hypotheses naming a weakness the Learner lacks. **Plan sources**: each Plan tallied `coach` / `retry` / `baseline`.
- **Convergence against the Baseline** (`convergence.ts`) — six Simulated Learners × 20 Sessions under the Coach and under a fixed-rule Baseline **on identical seeds**: Sessions to Mastery per Skill, first-try rate, and share of Problems whose *true* first-try probability sits in 0.7–0.9 (only a simulation knows that). Tuning (4) and held-out (2) splits reported apart.
- **Story and Summary validity, then the Judge** (`stories.ts`, `summaries.ts`) — 30 seeded Stories and 6 Parent Summaries pass deterministic validators first (first-attempt rate, rate within the bounded attempts, template fallbacks, rejection reasons by kind); only then does a rubric score readability and faithfulness.

Both Judge scores are **withheld unless the Judge agrees with a hand-labelled Calibration Set on ≥ 80 %** — 20 Stories (12 pass / 8 fail) and 10 Summaries (6 / 4), all already accepted by the validator, so the labels isolate exactly what a program cannot see (`calibration.ts`, `judge.ts:47`).

## 3. LLM-as-judge rigour: what the 80 % gate is worth

The threshold is well chosen in kind — MT-Bench reports that "strong LLM judges like GPT-4 can match both controlled and crowdsourced human preferences well, achieving over 80 % agreement, the same level of agreement between humans" (GPT-4 vs human 85 %, human vs human 81 %) [8]. The problem is the denominator and the labelling.

- **The interval swallows the gate.** A Judge scoring exactly 16/20 has a 95 % Wilson interval of **[0.58, 0.92]**; 8/10 gives **[0.49, 0.94]** — at n = 10, a Judge at the threshold is not distinguishable from a coin. Even a perfect 20/20 bounds true agreement only at ≥ 0.84. Pinning agreement to ±0.10 at p = 0.8 needs n ≈ 62; to ±0.05, n ≈ 246. (Wilson, not Wald, is the right interval here [9].)
- **Raw agreement flatters a lopsided set.** The Story set is 12 pass / 8 fail, so a Judge that always says "pass" scores **0.60** — three quarters of the way to the gate on no skill. That is Cohen's objection: percent agreement "overestimate[s] the true agreement among raters" [10]. At 80 % agreement with matching 60/40 marginals **κ = 0.58**: "weak" on McHugh's bands, and below her line that "any kappa below 0.60 indicates inadequate agreement" [10].
- **Below every published benchmark, and single-labelled.** promptfoo asks for 30–50 labelled examples, agreement **> 90 %**, and a **holdout set** to detect overfitting [5]; Anthropic says graders "should be closely calibrated with human experts" and that "20-50 simple tasks… is a great start" [11], with worked examples of 100–1,000 cases [12]. Ollie gates on 20 and 10 — all used for the gate, none held back — labelled by one person on 2026-09-13 (`calibration.ts:1-10`), with no human–human figure to give the ceiling. And it *judges* only 30 Stories and 6 Summaries. Six is not a measurement. EvalGen's finding is that this is exactly where evaluation breaks: "users need criteria to grade outputs, but grading outputs helps users define criteria" [13].
- **Same family.** Stories are Sonnet 5, Coach and Summaries Opus 5, Judge Opus 5 (`judge-anthropic.ts:22`). Anthropic: "generally best practice to use a different model to evaluate than the model used to generate the evaluated output" [12]. MT-Bench puts self-enhancement bias at ≈ +10 % for GPT-4 on its own output and ≈ +25 % for Claude-v1 [8]; a panel of disjoint families "outperforms a single large judge, exhibits less intra-model bias" [14], which Inspect ships as a grader panel with a majority reducer [2]. Ollie states the limitation and does not mitigate it.

## 4. Simulated learners and knowledge tracing

Ollie's engine runs Bayesian Knowledge Tracing with **hand-set** parameters (`loop/skills.ts`: `prior 0.15–0.3, learn 0.2, guess 0.15, slip 0.1`). Corbett and Anderson validated the original two ways: internally, predicted against actual error rates (r = 0.71–0.79 across three experiments), and externally, by predicting performance on a cumulative test taken **without tutor assistance** [18]. Since DKT the field's currency has been held-out AUC on public logs — 0.86 on ASSISTments against a best-prior 0.69, under 5-fold cross-validation [19]. Ollie does neither, because it has no logs: an honest consequence of the no-under-13-testing rule, but it means **nothing validates that the Simulated Learners resemble children.**

That benchmark is more fragile than it looks. Khajah et al. found "31.6% of [DKT's reported] difference in performance appears to be due to the use of a biased procedure for computing the AUC for BKT", and that "enhanced BKT appears to perform as well on average as DKT across the four data sets" [20]; Xiong et al. found **23.6 % of the ASSISTments rows are duplicates**, with DKT dropping 0.81 → 0.74 once they are removed [21]. Khan Academy's own EDM 2025 paper is sharpest: at AUC 0.81, **specificity stayed below 0.5** — the model predicts "correct" for items students actually got wrong, missing precisely the gaps you would intervene on [22]. Ollie's deterministic metrics are, in that light, closer to the decision than an AUC would be.

On the simulation itself, **not validating is the norm rather than Ollie's lapse**: Käser and Alexandron's systematic review finds that "almost half of the studies using simulated learners do not provide any evidence that their modeling addresses the most fundamental question in simulation design — is the model valid?" [23], and the LLM-simulation work says "surprisingly, little work has been done to ensure or even measure the quality of simulated students", reporting that "prompting strategies for student simulation perform poorly" and that simulators struggle to follow "cognitive characteristics of real human students, like **the power law of practice**" [24]. Ollie sidesteps that failure mode entirely — **no model plays the child**; answers are sampled from seeded per-Skill probabilities (`learners.ts:430-491`) — but inherits the other one: ability is **static by decision**, "a Simulated Learner does not improve with practice, so Sessions to Mastery measures how fast a planner confirms a fixed ability" (`.scratch/k5-math/decisions.md`, amendment 17). The chart therefore measures **diagnosis speed, not learning**, while "Sessions to Mastery" reads as a learning-gain claim; and with n = 4 tuning and n = 2 held-out Learners, 6.5 vs 6.0 and 5.0 vs 6.0 mean Skills Mastered are differences nobody can test.

**Where a comparable product's evaluation is public, it is behavioural, not rubric-based — and often thinner than its marketing.** Khan Academy A/B-tests Khanmigo in production, shipping only when the probability of beating control exceeds ".95 without any negative impact on guardrail metrics", on **next-item correctness** — "whether the student answers the next problem correctly after receiving tutoring", chosen for "independent learning transfer, not just of performance with AI assistance" — reporting +6.1 % across ~20 tests and ~15 million threads [25]; J-PAL is running a randomised trial with teachers assigned at random and standardised math scores as the outcome [26]. **Duolingo has published nothing on Birdbrain** [27]; its public spaced-repetition model reports AUC 0.538, *below* the Leitner baseline's 0.542, and was shipped on halved MAE plus an A/B test whose outcome was daily retention, not learning [28]. ASSISTments has the cleanest evidence — a school-level cluster RCT, 43 schools and 2,850 students, Hedges *g* = 0.18, WWC "Meets Standards Without Reservations" [29]; Carnegie Learning's WWC report is "mixed effects", and its best-known result was "no longer statistically significant" once adjusted for multiple comparisons [30]; Squirrel AI's headline effects come from company-authored two-day studies [31]. And in Ollie's own age band the bar is barely set: across 28 K-12 ITS studies, 14 % were elementary and none preschool [32].

## 5. Where Ollie is stronger than typical

1. **Deterministic first, Judge second.** Every Story and Summary passes a program before a model grades it, and the Calibration Set is built from items the validator already accepts — so the Judge is measured only on the residue a program cannot see. Most harnesses put the rubric first and the code check nowhere.
2. **Evidence Integrity is a deterministic faithfulness metric.** DeepEval and Ragas compute faithfulness as *supported claims ÷ total claims*, with an LLM doing both the decomposition and the verification [15][16]. Ollie's needs no model — a citation is right or wrong against the Log — and is scored over **rejected attempts too** (`hypotheses.ts:292-334`), so an invented Problem ID counts even though the engine refused the output. Every framework surveyed scores only what the system returned. This is the report's most reviewer-proof number: 1.00 over 2,331 citations.
3. **A control arm on identical seeds — the design Anthropic recommends.** The Baseline is the same pure Loop with a fixed rule on the same seed, so Diagnostic Sessions are byte-identical and every later difference is the planner's: the "analyze paired differences" advice, which "eliminates the variance in question difficulty" [17]. Eval frameworks compare variants of your own system; Ollie compares against a defensible null.
4. **A held-out split declared in advance**, scored separately in every report. No framework surveyed enforces one. **Byte-level reproducibility**: every draw is seeded by learner + Problem ID + attempt.
5. **A gate that withholds.** `judge.readability` and `judge.faithfulness` are `null` and the console says "scores withheld" when calibration fails; the `--fake` Judge fails by design, so every run demonstrates the gate. OpenAI Evals reports a metascore, promptfoo and LangSmith document a workflow — **none refuses to print the number** [1][5][6].

## 6. Where a rigorous reviewer would push

1. **The committed report is a `--fake` run.** All five reports in `docs/evals/` carry `coach.generation: "fake"`. The write-up says so honestly, but the harness has never produced a number about Claude. This is the first thing a reviewer checks.
2. **Calibration Sets of 20 and 10, single-labelled, no holdout** (§3): the interval is wider than the gate, κ at threshold is "weak", and the published benchmarks are 30–50 items at > 90 % [5].
3. **Detection by regex.** `namesWeakness` is a hand-written phrase list (`hypotheses.ts:162-178`) tested against ~10 developer-written strings. A Coach that writes "the jump past 10 is where it goes wrong" scores a miss: the metric measures phrasing as much as insight. `claimPolarity` has the same exposure, and it decides Evidence Integrity verdicts.
4. **Six hand-designed Learners, static ability, no practice effect**, no validation against real logs, n = 2 held out (§4).
5. **One run; nondeterminism unmeasured.** No `temperature` or seed is set on any call (`generation/anthropic.ts`, `judge-anthropic.ts`) and each Learner runs once — against Inspect's epochs [2], Braintrust's `trialCount` [7], and Anthropic's "a task that passed on one eval run might fail on the next" [11].
6. **No uncertainty anywhere.** 3/18 false positives is [0.06, 0.39]; 30/30 validity is [0.89, 1.00]; the report prints `1.00` and `16.7 %` [2][17].
7. **Same-family Judge; no real-child data; no CI** — the evals run by hand, so a regression is caught only if someone remembers.

## 7. Cheap improvements before the deadline (each ≤ a day)

- **Run `pnpm eval` live three times on the same seeds and commit all three reports.** One command fixes the biggest weakness (a fake headline) and the second biggest (no repeats), and yields a min–max spread per score for free.
- **Add Wilson 95 % intervals to `stats.ts`.** `share()` is already the single chokepoint for every rate; ~20 lines puts an interval on every number in the JSON and the console, calibration included [2][9][17].
- **Report κ, the confusion matrix and the trivial-judge baselines in `Calibration`.** Print `alwaysPass` / `alwaysFail` agreement (0.60 / 0.40 on the Story set) beside the Judge's, so a reader can see how much of the score is skill. Consider tightening the gate to "≥ 0.8 agreement **and** κ ≥ 0.6" [10].
- **Grow and balance the Calibration Sets to 30 Stories / 20 Summaries, 50/50 pass/fail, and hold 10 back** as an unseen check on the rubric — the shape promptfoo prescribes [5]. Half a day of labelling.
- **Add a second Judge from another family behind the existing `Judge` interface** (two methods, already a seam) and report both agreements — the cheapest possible answer to the self-enhancement objection [8][14].

## 8. Later

- **A practice effect in the Simulated Learners** — the power law of practice is exactly the cognitive characteristic simulators are faulted for missing [24] — so Sessions to Mastery measures learning rather than confirmation.
- **Calibrate the Learners against real response logs** (a public knowledge-tracing dataset, or the app's own anonymised Sessions once there are consenting users) and report how far simulated first-try rates sit from observed ones [33] — the validity evidence half the simulated-learner literature omits [23].
- **Fit the BKT parameters** instead of hand-setting them, and report held-out next-answer prediction on an item-split [19][34] — while remembering that AUC alone hid a specificity below 0.5 at Khan Academy [22].
- **Adopt a behavioural outcome once there are users**: next-item correctness after a Hint or a Coach-planned Session, which is what Khan Academy ships on [25]; and a jury of judges from disjoint families with a majority reducer [2][14].
- **Replace regex detection with a calibrated rubric** over ~100 labelled Coach claims, so the metric measures insight rather than vocabulary; and gate CI on the eval — a `--fake` run takes ~20 ms and would catch harness regressions on every PR.
